## What you are looking at

A neuron firing its neighbours and a late train delaying its connections are the same kind of event: one activation causes, on average, `σ` more. Both views above draw the same 160 node graph and run the same equations. Only the drawing changes.

When `σ` is below 1, cascades die out. Above 1, they grow until the network runs out of nodes that can respond. Useful systems often sit close to that edge, where a signal can travel but does not take over.

## What the controller does

A slow disturbance pushes the gain up and down every 16 seconds. A PI controller watches smoothed activity `A` and corrects the gain to hold the setpoint `A*`. Open the loop and the correction goes to zero: the network falls silent, then runs away, then falls silent again.

## The numbers

Each node activates each of its four out-neighbours with probability `σ/4`, so `σ` is normalised to one activation per activation, and critical gain `σc` is 1 for an ideal branching process. For isolated cascades on a large sparse network, mean cascade size is `1/(1-σ)`, and the test suite checks this model against that result to within 1%. The result does not hold exactly here: with 160 nodes and refractory periods, cascades collide, so the operating gain can sit above 1.

The dashed line is the setpoint and the band is 2% of full scale. With 16 of 160 nodes active, chance alone moves `A` by more than a 2% band around the setpoint would allow.
