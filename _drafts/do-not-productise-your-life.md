I recently watched a [podcast](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=t_NO1bK_boM) on which the guest was discussing how to apply [design thinking](https://www.interaction-design.org/literature/topics/design-thinking) to ones life. Now, I did see merits in the approach, such as approaching how to find your 'why' in a semi-structured way. Nonetheless, design thinking is a product-oriented approach, and the idea that you can design your life as though it were a product is something I am absolutely against. 

I'm stunned that applying design thinking to life planning is something so supposedly innovative that it grants a lectureship at Stanford. I'm equally stunned that the approach posed here is to apply design thinking rather than systems thinking to supposedly solve the wicked problem that is life planning. Design thinking implies that an innovative solution can be created to solve a problem that people face. A wicked problem is actually a concept from systems thinking, and they cannot actually be 'solved'. Rather, wicked problems are 'dissolved' - there is an ongoing effort to minimise negative externalities which the wicked problem would otherwise exacerbate. 

The world changes, and you change - change is the only constant. You have control over some elements in this system, others you don't. Hence, systems thinking is actually the better mental framework to apply to your life, which acknowledges the fascinating complexity of what it means to exist as a human being. We can then combine ideas from control theory (which talks about how to steer systems that are constantly changing into states we desire, continuously) so that we can manage this ongoing change. What does that look like in practice? Form ideas of where you want to get to (and most importantly why), form plans of how to get there, and reflect on both of these often. Your goals and paths to get there will change as you learn more and as your priorities shift. Life is a living, breathing and changing thing, and so an approach to guide you through life should also be living, breathing and changing. 

Want to engineer flow? Flow is defined as something with a dynamic rate of change. Start by thinking about where you want to get to, why, and consider 'what things do I need to continuously make progress on in order to get there'. This gives you an idea on what needs to flow that is grounded on a why. From there you consider habits and routines (control laws) to create these flow states which will guide you towards where you want to get to. 

Example
Goal: "I want to be healthy when I'm old"
Why: "because I want to maintain my health throughout my life"
Define the flow: "I need to build a habit of exercising x days/week" (note x days/week is the dynamic rate of change)
Control law: "I will go to the gym every morning on weekdays"
Result? You moved your exercise flow state from 0 to 5 days/week. 

I'm all for design thinking being used to solve problems at their core whilst not creating new ones. However, your life in itself is not a wicked problem. It is a continuously evolving process. Situations that require some form of action usually come from big changes that were hard or impossible to predict, sometimes for the good sometimes for the bad; and sometimes you had an influence in creating the situation in the first place. This is where design thinking falls down, because the premise is to 'design' or 'engineer' your life and then follow that plan makes no sense if the following week you get hit by a bus or win the lottery.
